# Investigations and design-review questions (owner, 2026-11-13)

**What this holds:** The 2026-11-13 investigations: hybrid rendering and ray tracing, culling and rasterization, wgpu, modularization, the shader toolchain and IR, and swappable and hybrid renderers. The owner's architecture decisions from the same discussion are in `docs/decisions/2026-11-13-architecture-decisions.md`. Moved from the overall plan (`CESIUM2_WEBGPU_PLAN.md`) on 2026-11-13, with its content and dates unchanged.

Recorded to explore, nothing decided. Each item gives the question and a first take.

**A design principle for the API review: the surface leaves room to innovate underneath.**

- Every public type should say what it promises and what it leaves to the engine, and the docs should say so plainly.
- The test case is swapping the renderer. Could a ray-traced renderer, a WebGL 2 renderer, a future API, or a browser- or hardware-specific feature (an extension exposed to WebGPU) sit behind the same `Engine`, `World` and `View`, with fewer features where it must?
- First take: the existing engine profile (the shading and compatibility tiers) might grow into a renderer profile: capabilities a view asks for and a backend reports, with the engine choosing per view. Do a prior-art search first: Unreal's RHI and feature levels, Filament's backends, bgfx, wgpu's downlevel flags, Hydra render delegates in USD (the closest match: one scene, swappable renderers, each declaring what it supports), and OSPRay and Embree's split.

**Hybrid rendering and ray tracing.**

- Is it better to composite separate renderers (a path tracer beside the WebGPU rasterizer, joined by depth and IDs), or to have one renderer with ray queries in raster shaders, as RenderMan and DXR inline ray tracing do?
- First take:
  - Compositing is cheap to build, but it splits lighting (no shared shadows or reflections) and doubles memory.
  - Inline ray queries are the better fit for hybrid effects.
  - A path tracer as a separate renderer is a good test of the swap above.
  - A public ray-query abstraction ([ray-queries-and-path-tracing.md](ray-queries-and-path-tracing.md), [ray-queries-where-they-run.md](ray-queries-where-they-run.md) already have one on the CPU and GPU) could let a path tracer live in the application.
- Owner's lead, from OptiX: compile a renderer from kernels. The IR ([transform-graph.md](transform-graph.md), [styling-and-a-shared-ir.md](styling-and-a-shared-ir.md)) and the raster pipeline's stages could be extended so raster and ray stages are composed and specialized together: programs per hit kind, linked and specialized at build or run time.

**GPU culling and rasterization.**

- **Meshlet and cluster culling with a depth pyramid:** frustum, cone (backface) and occlusion tests per cluster of about 64–128 triangles in compute, ideally with subgroup operations. These apply to dense design models and photogrammetry. They need clusters built at load or in the tiler (meshoptimizer), and they build on fix 7's pyramid (the transform-graph plan's Task 12).
  - The risk is subgroup availability: Safari and Firefox have about none ([risks.md](risks.md), "Platform and WebGPU risks"). Keep a portable path.
- **Software rasterization in compute** for small triangles and vector marks, as Nanite does for clusters whose triangles are about a pixel. Vector rendering already does some of this. The question is how one compute rasterizer serves meshes, vectors and points, writing visibility through 64-bit atomics (depth and ID packed).
- **64-bit atomics:** WebGPU has no 64-bit atomics today, and there's a proposal to track. Without them, the packed depth-and-ID trick needs two passes or 32-bit packing. List what else they would help: order-independent transparency lists, splat sorting keys, ray-query hit merging, and histograms.

**Shader toolchain and IR: think the combinations through before building more (owner's questions).**

- **Material languages:**
  - OSL: probably offline-only, but its closures as a material model are worth learning from.
  - MaterialX: its node graphs as an interchange we read. It already targets GLSL, MSL and others, so we could take its generated code or translate its graph into our IR.
  - Other open standards to consider: OpenPBR and glTF's material extensions.
- **The IR:**
  - Naga's IR is portable and has a WASM build. It's wgpu's, so it isn't the browser's compiler.
  - Tint has an internal IR, not a stable public one.
  - WGSL itself is the only thing portable across every WebGPU implementation, so whatever the internal IR is, the engine's output to the browser is WGSL.
  - Question: should the engine's own small IR (`src/transform/ir.ts`) stay as the place for autodiff and intervals and emit WGSL, with naga only for validation and the GLSL fallback?
- **Slang for everything?**
  - Styling and vector stages as Slang interfaces and generics: operator and generator stages as implementations, linked and specialized when each module is linked.
  - The 3D Tiles 1.x styling language compiled to a Slang module at runtime.
  - The internal linker on Slang at build time, and Slang's GLSL target for a WebGL 2 backend.
  - The cost is the Slang WASM binary in the core, which could be lazy-loaded when styling or custom shaders are used, as custom shaders are today.
- **WESL** for the internal shader linker instead of our own, with naga only for the GLSL path.
- **Inputs:** many styling inputs (GML, the 3D Tiles styling language, a new styling spec, SVG). What does the engine's API take: a language, an IR, or both, so new languages can be added without core changes?
- **Needs:** a deep comparison of the combinations (Slang only; Slang plus our IR; WESL plus naga; our IR to WGSL plus naga), covering bundle size, compile latency, portability, autodiff, specialization and a WebGL 2 path. Run it as a research session before [styling-language.md](styling-language.md)'s spikes.

**Targeting wgpu instead of WebGPU directly.**

- Gains: wgpu brings native builds and a WebGL 2 fallback (its downlevel tiers) behind one API.
- Costs: shipping a WASM wgpu in the browser (size, a second validation layer, and losing the browser's own WebGPU optimizations), and Rust in the build.
- First take: the browser path stays on native WebGPU. wgpu becomes interesting for a native or headless engine (M6's "headless via Dawn" is the alternative), or as the WebGL 2 backend behind the renderer-profile seam above.

**Modularization ([modular-renderer.md](modular-renderer.md)).**

- File formats as their own modules: GeoJSON, KML, CZML, glTF, USD and more, each a loader behind one content interface, loaded on demand.
- The Slang interface as its own module too, if the API's shader input becomes an IR.

## Shader toolchain and IR: the four combinations compared (research, 2026-11-13)

The owner's question above, worked through before [styling-language.md](styling-language.md)'s spikes. Nothing is decided here. Measurements were made on this machine (Apple M2, Node 24.19, the store's Slang 2026.21); outside facts give the page and the date read. Scripts were scratch files and aren't in the repository.

**What is there today.**

- _Engine shaders:_ 47 `.slang` files, compiled offline by the pinned `slangc` to checked-in WGSL (`npm run shaders`), with reflection feeding the TypeScript layouts.
- _Custom shaders:_ `CustomShader.fromSlang` compiles user Slang at run time with the WASM build in a worker, loaded on first use (`slang.ts`, `slangWorker.ts`); `checkBundle` guards that it stays out of the main chunk.
- _The internal linker:_ `src/shaders/link` (about 850 lines) joins checked WGSL units across declared boundaries, with a `Linker` interface that already names Slang and WESL-style imports as possible strategies.
- _Styles:_ `styleExpression.ts` compiles 1.x expressions straight to WGSL strings; `VectorStyle.ts` reuses it for point layers. Neither goes through an IR.
- _Transforms:_ `src/transform` parses restricted JS into `ir.ts` (hash-consed scalar DAG), with forward-mode AD, interval evaluation, Taylor bounds, a float64 CPU evaluator, and WGSL and df64 emitters.

**Measured here.**

| What                                                           | Result                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Slang WASM binary (`slang-wasm.wasm`, 2026.21)                 | 25.2 MB raw, 10.4 MB gzip -9, 7.8 MB brotli -q 11; its JS glue 155 KB raw, 36 KB brotli       |
| Slang WASM start (Node; a browser's streaming compile differs) | instantiate 36–49 ms, `createGlobalSession` 146–199 ms                                        |
| A 1.x style as a Slang module, to WGSL                         | first compile 270 ms, then 34–49 ms each (median of 10, new session each, as the worker does) |
| The same, to GLSL                                              | 29–53 ms each; the output is `#version 450` whatever profile is asked (see below)             |
| The same style through `styleExpression.ts`, to WGSL           | about 10 µs each (median of 2,000), 1 ms the first time                                       |
| A JS twist through `parseTransform`, its Jacobian, to WGSL     | about 34 µs each, all three steps                                                             |
| Pipeline creation for a new shader (the browser's compiler)    | 50–110 ms per new shader, as `ShaderCache.ts` states (not re-measured here)                   |

So a front end on our IR costs microseconds and Slang tens of milliseconds, three to four orders of magnitude apart; both sit beside the browser's own pipeline compile, which costs about as much as Slang's warm compile.

**Five facts that change the question.**

1. _A WebGL 2 backend is a second renderer, not a translation._ [layers-engine-renderer-backend.md](layers-engine-renderer-backend.md) found that WebGL 2 has no compute, storage buffers or indirect draws, so the GPU-driven renderer's shaders can't be translated to it by any compiler. What could be shared with a WebGL 2 renderer is function-level code: a style's fields, a transform's function, a material's evaluation. So the criterion is "can the style, transform and material functions be emitted as GLSL ES 3.00", not "can the engine's modules".
2. _Slang's GLSL target isn't GLSL ES._ Asked for GLSL, the WASM build emits `#version 450` with `layout(column_major) buffer;`, and `findProfile` knows no ES profile (`glsl_300_es`, `glsl_es_300`, `glsl_310_es` all return 0; `glsl_330` is known and still gives 450). WebGL 2 would need a second step (Slang to WGSL to naga's GLSL ES writer, or Slang to SPIR-V to SPIRV-Cross).
3. _Slang in the browser has no CPU target._ The WASM build's targets are GLSL, HLSL, WGSL, SPIR-V, Metal and CUDA. Picks, measurements, legends, queries and accuracy need the same function on the CPU in float64 ([transform-graph.md](transform-graph.md)'s "truth on the CPU"), which only a JS evaluator of our own gives.
4. _Slang's generics can carry the IR's interpretations on the GPU._ A function generic over an `INum` interface compiled for both a float and an interval implementation, and `fwd_diff` of a `[Differentiable]` function, both went to WGSL in the WASM build (200 ms cold). So AD and interval evaluation on the GPU aren't a reason by themselves to keep our IR; the CPU side and the latency are.

5. _Slang's own WGSL target is still called a work in progress._ Its WGSL page lists what it can't do (wave intrinsics, 64-bit and float atomics, `SV_PrimitiveID`, `SV_ClipDistance`), and 69 issues labeled WebGPU are open, among them WGSL that only naga accepts, with no diagnostic (#13914), and no way to express compatibility mode (#13933). It has been fine for the engine's kernels offline, where `shaders:check` and the browser test what it emits; it's a larger bet for code generated at run time from data.

**The four combinations.** In each, the engine's own shaders can stay Slang compiled offline; what differs is what happens at run time and where styles, transforms and vector fields live.

_(1) Slang for everything._ Styling and vector stages are Slang interfaces; a style, a 1.x style or a transform is lowered by a front end to a Slang module and linked with `extern`/`export` against precompiled engine modules; the WASM compiler loads lazily the first time anything is styled; GLSL for WebGL 2 from Slang's GLSL target.

- For: one language from engine kernels to user code, with interfaces, generics, link-time specialization and autodiff that work in the WASM build (measured above). Precompiled IR modules cut the per-compile front-end work. Khronos governance. MaterialX's new Slang generator gives material graphs a direct way in.
- Against: every styled scene pays 7.8 MB (brotli) and about half a second of compiler start before its first style, and 35–50 ms per style edit on top of the pipeline compile. No CPU target, so picks, legends, queries and interval culling still need a JS evaluator with the same semantics, which is most of option 2's IR anyway. Its GLSL isn't GLSL ES, so WebGL 2 needs a second translator. A front end is still needed per language (1.x expressions aren't Slang). A full compiler runs on code generated from third-party tileset data. `checkBundle`'s guard that styling needs no Slang would go.

_(2) Slang for engine shaders, our IR for what's generated at run time._ Today's model, finished: engine kernels in Slang offline; styles, transforms and vector fields lowered by front ends to `ir.ts` (grown with types, vectors, input kinds and rates, [styling-and-a-shared-ir.md](styling-and-a-shared-ir.md) spike 1) and emitted as WGSL at the WGSL-level ABI ([shaders.md](shaders.md)); runtime Slang stays lazy, for custom shaders only.

- For: the core pays nothing for a compiler, and a style compiles in microseconds. One semantic core for GPU code, the float64 CPU evaluator, AD, intervals, Taylor bounds and df64. The IR's JSON form can be the spec's interchange (§13.38), and cesium-native implements a few dozen ops, not a compiler. A GLSL ES emitter is about the size of the WGSL one (`wgsl.ts`, 264 lines), and expression-level code is exactly what fact 1 says WebGL 2 would share.
- Against: we own an IR, which is the wheel the owner doesn't want to reinvent; it must grow types, structs, rates and perhaps bounded loops, carefully. Two worlds meet at the ABI, whose exported names Slang mangles (`records_0`), so `boundary.ts` and its tests keep the seam honest. Built-in transforms are written twice (Slang with `[Differentiable]`, mirrored in TS, [transform-graph.md](transform-graph.md) decision 2). The emitted WGSL is checked only by the browser today.

_(3) WESL as the internal linker, naga only for GLSL._ The `Linker` strategy becomes WESL's runtime `link()` (imports, `@if`, constants); generated styles become WESL modules; `naga-wasm` loads with a WebGL 2 renderer to write GLSL ES.

- For: a community format for WGSL modules, with bundler plugins, npm packages and a runtime linker in the browser; conditional compilation we'd otherwise write; Slang's WGSL output is valid input.
- Against: no 1.0 and no generics yet, so no interface-based specialization like Slang's. It answers none of the IR questions: no AD, intervals or CPU evaluation. It replaces 850 lines of linker that check our boundaries' signatures, which WESL doesn't know about (not checked whether it can). naga's GLSL ES can't take compute or storage buffers (fact 1 again), and its validation isn't Tint's.
- _(3a), the owner's variant (2026-11-13):_ Slang (offline) emits `.wgsl` helper modules; the entry points that need WGSL Slang can't write stay hand-written as `.wesl` files importing them; WESL's `link()` (JS or Rust) joins them into one WGSL module. This is the pattern the engine already runs with its own linker: since step 5 (r56) `surface.slang` compiles to WGSL and `surfaceVertex.wgsl` holds the vertex entry points by hand, because Slang emits no `@invariant` (the opaque pass tests for depth equal to the prepass's), joined across `SURFACE_VERTEX_BOUNDARY`. So (3a) swaps only the joining step: our signature checks at boundaries against WESL's imports and `@if`. Limit: WESL writes WGSL only, so a WebGL 2 renderer still runs the linked WGSL through naga for GLSL ES 3.00, with naga's limits above. Question for the spike: can a WESL import carry our boundary's checked signature, or does a test keep checking it?

_(4) Our IR to WGSL, naga for validation and GLSL._ As (2), but no Slang at run time (`CustomShader` takes WGSL, or IR, only), with `naga-wasm` validating every emitted WGSL module in CI and writing GLSL ES for a WebGL 2 renderer.

- For: the smallest runtime (about 2 MB unpacked for naga, only with WebGL 2); emitted WGSL validated in CI against a second implementation; a maintained GLSL ES writer for whole modules.
- Against: drops `CustomShader.fromSlang`, which [shaders.md](shaders.md) made public API. The wrapper is third-party and young (build our own from the crate, with Rust in the build, if it matters). Translating WGSL to GLSL ES hits the same ES 300 limits. Two validators that disagree on uniformity need a rule for which one wins.

**What the engine's API should accept.** Both, in layers, so a new language never touches the core:

- _The IR is the contract_ for styles, transforms and vector fields: typed nodes with declared inputs, kinds and rates ([styling-language.md](styling-language.md)'s runtime contract), with a JSON form that is versioned like a glTF extension. The engine's API takes an IR program.
- _Languages are front-end modules_ that lower to it: the 1.x expressions, restricted JS functions, the new styling language, and later GML or SLD-style rules, SVG presentation attributes and MapLibre expressions. Each is its own entry point, loaded on demand ([modular-renderer.md](modular-renderer.md)), and none is in the core.
- _Shader code stays the escape hatch_ at the WGSL-level ABI: Slang (lazy) or WGSL for what the IR can't say. A style that needs more than the IR is a custom shader, not a bigger IR.
- An IR to Slang emitter is cheap insurance: where a custom shader already loaded the compiler, a style could be linked into its Slang module, and option 1 stays open without rewriting front ends.

**MaterialX, OpenPBR and OSL.**

- _OpenPBR_ is the material model to compare the engine's against (versioned, with a reference in MaterialX); the comparison is its own task.
- _MaterialX_ meets us in two places. Its node graphs are material functions: pattern nodes map onto the IR's ops one by one, which makes a graph a front end. Its generated code is shader text: the Slang generator plus `slangc` offline turns a `.mtlx` into an engine material at build time, and its ESSL generator serves a WebGL 2 renderer. Its "WGSL" generator still needs naga or Tint.
- _OSL_ has no GPU back end outside OptiX, so it's a model to learn from (closures as the material's result), not a target.

**Recommendation (to take or leave).** Option 2 now, with the pieces of 4 that cost nothing at run time:

1. Engine kernels stay Slang offline, as today.
2. `ir.ts` becomes the runtime core for styles, transforms and vector fields, as [styling-and-a-shared-ir.md](styling-and-a-shared-ir.md) and [styling-language.md](styling-language.md) planned; its JSON form is the interface the API takes.
3. Runtime Slang stays lazy, for custom shaders only, and `checkBundle` keeps styling free of it.

6. WESL isn't adopted now. The `Linker` interface keeps the door open; look again at its 1.0 or when it has generics.
7. Revisit option 1 if any of these changes: Slang ships a CPU target in its WASM build, its WASM size falls by an order of magnitude, or the IR grows loops, structs and memory ([styling-and-a-shared-ir.md](styling-and-a-shared-ir.md)'s "revisit if").

## Swappable and hybrid renderers behind `Engine`, `World` and `View` (research, 2026-11-13)

This works through four of the questions above: the renderer swap, hybrid rendering, the owner's OptiX lead, and wgpu. It's research, and nothing is decided. Code facts are from a read-only survey of `packages/next` on this date. Outside facts were read on 2026-11-13; "undated" means the page shows no date. The shader-toolchain comparison (Slang, WESL, naga, our IR) is a separate research session and isn't repeated here.

**Short answers.**

1. _Swapping the renderer:_ yes for a path tracer, a WebGL 2 renderer and browser- or hardware-specific features, provided the seam is a **renderer** behind each view, not a GPU **backend** under one renderer ([layers-engine-renderer-backend.md](layers-engine-renderer-backend.md) drew this line). Hydra's render delegates are the model. The engine has no "profile" today, only device facts (`Capabilities`, `LimitsTier`) and one per-view quality tier (glare). Those are backend facts. A renderer has to report what it supports in the scene's terms: content kinds, view options, outputs and queries.
2. _Hybrid:_ one renderer with ray queries inside its passes is the better fit for effects (shadows, reflections, ambient occlusion). Compositing a separate renderer fits a whole view drawn another way, such as a progressive path-traced view. A path tracer can live in the application only if the public ray-query API gets a GPU side, a shader interface that a user's compute stage calls. Today's `SpatialQueries` is batched, asynchronous and read back on the CPU: right for line of sight, far too slow for 10⁶ rays per tick.
3. _The OptiX lead:_ most of OptiX's pieces already exist here under other names (draw records as SBT records, passes as ray types, route shapes as specialization keys). The half worth taking first is writing material, style and clip code once as stage-agnostic modules, so a raster pass and a ray kernel link the same source. The hard part is placement: smooth routes push vertices forward easily but rays need the inverse.
4. _wgpu:_ not for the browser. On browser WebGPU, wgpu is a thin binding and buys nothing; its WebGL 2 backend has no compute shaders, so it can't run this GPU-driven renderer either. For headless and Node, Dawn's `webgpu` package gives the same JavaScript API without Rust.

### One API, several renderers

**Two seams, again.** Every system above but Hydra and OSPRay swaps the API under one renderer. That's [layers-engine-renderer-backend.md](layers-engine-renderer-backend.md)'s GPU backend, and it only helps an API with the same abilities. A ray-traced renderer, a WebGL 2 renderer and this GPU-driven rasterizer are different algorithms, so they meet at Hydra's seam: one scene, a renderer per view, each saying what it draws.

**Where the seam would sit here.** The pieces line up already:

- _Scene in:_ `WorldScene` and `EngineScene` are renderer-neutral by rule, and content writes through `DrawSink`. A second renderer would take a world's draws, materials, transforms and lights from these, as a Hydra delegate takes prims from the scene index.
- _Image and answers out:_ a view's outputs (HDR color, display color, feature IDs, depth) are Hydra's AOVs in all but name. Picks, `pickPosition` and `SpatialQueries` are the answers an application relies on most, and they must work, more slowly if need be, whatever draws the view.
- _The renderer:_ today everything under `EngineRaster` and `WorldRaster`. A second one would sit beside them, chosen per view.

**The candidates, one by one.**

| Candidate                                     | Seam                                                                                                                   | What it would lack                                                                                                                                                                                                 | What the public API must not promise                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Path tracer (progressive)                     | Renderer per view, over the same arena and tables ([ray-queries-and-path-tracing.md](ray-queries-and-path-tracing.md)) | Real-time updates (it converges over ticks); per-tick effects such as auto-exposure history; perhaps vectors and labels, which it would composite rather than trace                                                | That a view's image is final after one tick. A "converged" or "samples so far" signal belongs to the view |
| WebGL 2 renderer                              | Renderer per view, its own chunk ([layers-engine-renderer-backend.md](layers-engine-renderer-backend.md) option 3)     | Compute, storage buffers, indirect draws: CPU culling, one draw per record or batch, fewer lights, no GPU ray queries, no subgroups; the 43 compute kernels need CPU or fragment equivalents                       | WGSL in user shaders (`CustomShader.wgsl`), `GpuDevice`, `PostStage` written as WGSL                      |
| A future API, or native through webgpu.h      | Backend: same abilities, so the renderer is unchanged                                                                  | Nothing, if it is WebGPU-shaped (Dawn and wgpu-native share `webgpu.h`)                                                                                                                                            | Browser-only types in core signatures (`HTMLCanvasElement` as the only target)                            |
| Browser- or hardware-specific WebGPU features | Device facts inside one renderer, as feature levels are in Unreal                                                      | The faster path where a feature is missing: subgroups (none in Safari and Firefox, [architecture.md](architecture.md), "GPU layer (gpu/)"), `shader-f16`, 64-bit min and max atomics, ray queries, resource tables | That a feature exists everywhere. **Features worth tracking, with their state on 2026-11-13.**

- _64-bit atomics:_ the `atomic-64-min-max` proposal (an `atomic_vec2u_min_max` feature: `atomicStoreMin` and `atomicStoreMax` on `atomic<vec2u>`, no return value) is merged into the gpuweb repository as a proposal ([PR #11080](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/pull/11080), 2026-10-30; [proposal text](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/proposals/atomic-64-min-max.md)), motivated by virtual-geometry software rasterization. Dawn has an experimental Vulkan implementation. Whether stable Chrome ships it wasn't found. It is exactly the packed depth-and-ID write above's software-rasterization item asks for.
- _Ray tracing:_ an acceleration-structure proposal ([gpuweb PR #11054](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/pull/11054), opened 2026-10-13, open) and a WGSL ray-query proposal ([PR #11056](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/pull/11056), opened 2026-10-13; the WGSL group resolved on 2026-10-27 to accept it as a proposal). Both descend from [issue #535](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/issues/535). Reviewers note that wgpu already implements ray queries natively. It's inline ray queries, the DXR 1.1 model, not OptiX-style pipelines.

**Is a profile the right seam?** Partly. There is no profile today. There are device facts, a limits tier and one quality tier, and they answer "what can the GPU do". A renderer swap needs a second layer that answers "what will this view do":

- _Device facts_ stay as they are, mostly internal: an ordered set of tiers in Unreal's or Filament's manner, for choosing paths inside the WebGPU renderer.
- _A renderer report_ is public and stated in the scene's terms, as Hydra's lists are:
  - content kinds it draws (meshes, instanced, billboards, points, vectors, patches and b-reps, terrain, sky);
  - view options it honors (`shadows`, `ambientOcclusion`, `translucencyMethod`, post stages by slot);
  - outputs (HDR color, display color, IDs, depth);
  - queries it answers on its own GPU work, and which fall back to the CPU backends;
  - how it updates: each tick, or progressively.
- A view asks for what it needs, the engine picks a renderer per view, and a request a renderer can't meet is reported (an event and a read-only `view.support`), never dropped in silence. Glare's `"auto"` tier is today's example of the engine choosing quietly; it should be visible too.

### Composing a renderer from kernels (the owner's OptiX lead)

**OptiX's model, in brief.** OptiX 7 ([2019-11-20 introduction](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-to-get-started-with-optix-7); released at SIGGRAPH 2019) compiles PTX into modules, groups programs into program groups (ray generation, miss, exception, callables, and hit groups of intersection, any hit and closest hit) and links the groups into a pipeline. The shader binding table (SBT) is an array of records, each a program header and the application's data; traversal picks a record from the instance's offset, the geometry's index and the ray type. The application writes programs; OptiX owns traversal, scheduling and specialization of the linked whole. OptiX 9 adds a clusters API (RTX Mega Geometry) and cooperative vectors ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/neural-rendering-in-nvidia-optix-using-cooperative-vectors), undated; release date not confirmed, early 2025 by press reports).

**The engine has most of the pieces under other names.**

| OptiX                         | Here today                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SBT record (program and data) | A draw record and the material table: the program is the pipeline of the record's batch; the data is placement, arena offsets, material index, feature-ID base and imagery slice                                                                                                                                             |
| Ray type in the SBT index     | The pass: opaque, prepass, depth, shadow, pick. Each draw has a pipeline per pass, as each hit group has a record per ray type                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Hit group per geometry kind   | Batch kinds: mesh, instanced, billboard, patch, points, vectors, each with its own vertex entry                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| Closest hit                   | The surface: material, lighting, the user's `fragmentMain`, the style's color                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Any hit                       | Alpha mask, clips, the style's `show`: `discard` in the fragment stage                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| Miss                          | The sky and atmosphere passes                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| Ray generation                | The camera, including a lens-distortion node of the transform graph                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Link-time specialization      | One fused function per route shape ([transform-graph.md](transform-graph.md)), a pipeline per route variant (`ShaderCache`), and Slang's [link-time specialization](https://shader-slang.org/slang/user-guide/link-time-specialization.html) (`extern` and `export` types and constants across precompiled modules; undated) |

**What it would look like.** A program table keyed by what a draw is, with an entry per stage kind:

- _Key:_ (geometry kind, material model, route shape, style).
- _Stage kinds:_ `place` (the route from object to render space, and its Jacobian for normals), `cover` (raster: vertex and coverage; rays: intersection), `accept` (mask, clips, `show`), `shade` (surface and style color), and two outside any draw, `generate` (the camera) and `miss` (the sky).
- _A raster pass_ links `place`, `cover`, `accept` and `shade` into a vertex and a fragment entry, as today.
- _A ray kernel_ links `generate`, traversal, `cover` as intersection, `accept`, `shade` and `miss`.
- `accept` and `shade` are the same source in both. A material, a style or a clip is written once and drawn either way. That is the first half worth building, and it costs little now: keep raster-only built-ins (`front_facing`, derivatives, `sample_mask`, the varyings' layout) out of material and style code, behind the boundary the linker already checks.

**The hard part is `place`.** Raster pushes vertices forward through the route. A ray has to go the other way, or the geometry has to be placed before traversal:

- _Affine routes_ invert exactly: carry the ray into object space, as every ray tracer does for instances.
- _Smooth routes_ (exaggeration, unrolled alignments, lens distortion) have no cheap inverse. Either build a BLAS from placed vertices, one per route shape (as twins are one draw per route shape today, [transform-graph.md](transform-graph.md)), or intersect in the domain with the forward map and its Jacobian (Newton on the hit, started from the route's interval bounds). The first costs memory per route shape; the second costs time but is exact to the route's declared accuracy.
- The transform graph's IR is the home for `place` in both: it already emits the forward function, J and intervals. An inverse-by-Newton emitter would be one more back end, and the CPU's float64 version is the truth for refining hits, as it is for picks.

**WebGPU has no indirect calls.** OptiX links continuation calls and callables. WGSL has neither, so a ray kernel reaches a program in one of two ways:

- _Megakernel:_ a `switch` on the record's program ID inside one kernel. Simple, but divergent on wide GPUs, and every new program recompiles the kernel.
- _Wavefront:_ sort hits by program ID and run one small kernel per program over its queue, with indirect dispatch. More passes and memory, less divergence, and a new program adds a kernel without recompiling the others.
- The raster side already took the second shape: one pipeline per (batch kind, shader, route variant), drawn indirectly from lists that compute culled. A ray side would mirror it.

**Costs.**

- _Pipeline count:_ each new key multiplies pipelines (a style makes 4 today, more with translucency and double-sided content). Specialize where it pays, and keep a generic path driven by uniforms, as the style-constants note suggests.
- _Compile latency:_ 50–110 ms per new shader, in the background. A run-time linker has to stay off the critical path, as pipelines do now (draws whose pipeline isn't ready are skipped).
- _Slang in the browser_ is 7.7 MB brotli ([m0-spike-results.md](../history/m0-spike-results.md)). Composing at build time, and at run time only for user code, keeps it out of the core.

### Questions for the API review

1. Does each view report what it does (`view.support`), and does an unmet request warn, throw or fall back? Today some fall back silently (glare's `"auto"`, direct draws without `indirect-first-instance`).
2. Should the public API name a view's outputs (color, display color, IDs, depth) as AOVs, and accept an outside color and depth to composite?
3. Is the user shader contract a WGSL ABI ([shaders.md](shaders.md)), a Slang interface, or an IR? Only the last two can reach a second renderer; a WGSL string can't.
4. Do `GpuDevice`, `Capabilities` and `LimitsTier` stay in the main entry point, or move to an advanced entry for hosts that share a device?
5. Should `Engine.queries` take backends (as `createSpatialQueries` does), so an application or a second renderer supplies its own?
6. Is a GPU-side ray query (a shader interface plus a user compute slot) part of 2.0's surface, or a later addition?
7. Is a progressive view (one that improves over ticks) a concept in the API, with a "converged" signal and samples so far?
8. Which content kinds must every renderer draw, and which may it leave out (vectors and labels for a path tracer; patches and b-reps for WebGL 2)?
9. Do browser- and hardware-specific paths show in the API at all, or only in the report and the inspector?

### Decisions (owner, 2026-11-13)

1. **Support reports: yes.** Each view reports what it supports in the scene's terms (`view.support`: content kinds, view options, outputs, queries). An unmet request warns once and falls back as documented; a strict mode throws, for tests and careful hosts. Today's silent fallbacks (glare's `"auto"`, direct draws without `indirect-first-instance`) become reported ones.
2. **Outputs as AOVs: yes.** Name a view's outputs (color, display color, IDs, depth) now. Taking an outside color and depth to composite comes later, under the same names.
3. **The user shader contract: to be discussed with the shader toolchain research.** WGSL at the interface probably isn't right: a WGSL string can't reach a second renderer.
4. **`GpuDevice`, `Capabilities` and `LimitsTier` move to an advanced entry point** (`@cesium/next/gpu`); the main API stays renderer-agnostic (the worker mode needs this too: a shared device can't cross threads). **Open design:** the broader engine-profile design, so that support reports, device facts, tiers and presets don't become a fragile system. Design it before adding more tiers. **Planned:** [`docs/superpowers/plans/2026-11-13-engine-profiles.md`](../superpowers/plans/2026-11-13-engine-profiles.md), reviewed and decided by the owner on 2026-11-13. The app owns the look and the engine owns fidelity; features have short ladders of steps, including render resolution, MSAA and aerial perspective; rules are data; and an opt-in governor changes only render scale.
5. **`Engine.queries` takes backends: yes,** so an application or a second renderer can supply its own.
6. **GPU-side ray queries: later.** Design the module boundary so it can be added; don't ship it in 2.0 unless a 2.0 feature needs it.
7. **Progressive views: yes, as a special case of a general concept (proposed, to confirm): refinement.** A view's image converges over ticks; refinement sources each report progress and a done state: streaming (tiles loading), transitions (geomorph, imagery fades), async shader compiles (placeholder pipelines), occlusion feedback reaching its fixed point, temporal accumulation (TAA, still-frame supersampling for screenshots and print), a progressive path tracer's samples. `view.refinement` lists the active sources; `view.converged(options)` resolves when they are done (screenshots, printing, tests, benchmarks, exports); the on-demand loop ticks while any source is active, replacing the ad-hoc `TICK_REASONS`. A progressive view is a view with an accumulation source.
8. **Content every renderer must draw:** globe, terrain, imagery and meshes (3D Tiles, glTF). Optional but declared in `view.support`: vectors and labels, points and splats, b-rep patches, advanced translucency. Design this carefully with item 4.
9. **Browser- and hardware-specific paths stay out of the API:** they show in the support report and the inspector only. Where such a path can run, the engine detects it automatically and uses it.
